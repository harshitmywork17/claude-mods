Bigram Joint Distribution

A language model has to say how likely a whole sentence is. Start with the smallest sentence that still has order: two words.

1The problem

We want one number for every pair of words: how likely is it that the previous word is \(v_i\) and the next word is \(v_j\)? With a vocabulary of five words there are \(5 \times 5 = 25\) such pairs, and their chances must add up to one.

2The idea: a table of chances

A joint distribution gives the chance of two things happening together, not one after the other.

Write the two word positions as random variables \(\inp{W_{t-1}}\) and \(\outp{W_t}\). Their joint distribution is a function from a pair of indices to a probability:

\[ \pr{P_{ij}} \;=\; \fn{p}\big(\inp{W_{t-1}} = \inp{v_i},\; \outp{W_t} = \outp{v_j}\big), \qquad \sum_{i}\sum_{j} \pr{P_{ij}} = 1 . \]

Because \(i\) and \(j\) each run over five words, the whole function fits in a \(5 \times 5\) matrix. Hover a cell of the table or a bar of the plot: they are the same number.

Insight
The row sums form the distribution of the previous word alone, the marginal \(\fn{p}(\inp{W_{t-1}} = \inp{v_i})\). Here every row sums to 0.20, so on its own each word is equally likely to come first.

2.1Sharpening the table

Raise every entry to a power \(\prm{\tau}\) and renormalise. Larger \(\prm{\tau}\) piles the probability onto the likeliest pair.
